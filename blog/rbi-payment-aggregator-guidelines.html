RBI payment aggregator guidelines for choosing an Indian checkout partner

Use the RBI payment aggregator guidelines to check who onboards your business, handles customer funds and explains settlement before signing a contract.

AQ Pay Payments Team·17 November 2026·5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Ask for the legal names of the technology provider and the entity responsible for collecting and settling funds.
  • Identify who reviews the merchant and who approves a change of settlement account.
  • Before signing, trace a purchase and a partial refund using the evidence each partner can actually provide.

An Indian retailer comparing two checkout proposals might see its own logo on both screens and the same payment methods on both price sheets. Those details say little about who collects the customer’s money or who can explain a missing settlement. The RBI payment aggregator guidelines give the retailer a better starting question: which legal entity performs each part of this sale?

Use a domestic online purchase to brief a prospective merchant services provider. Bring a sample order, the proposed merchant agreement and the legal name on the receiving bank account. The aim of the meeting is to leave with a named owner for onboarding, collection, settlement and support. Here is how to run it.

Read the RBI payment aggregator guidelines against your own contract

The RBI’s 2025 Master Direction brings online, physical and cross-border payment aggregation into one framework. Its definition of a payment aggregator includes collecting customer payments and subsequently settling the funds to merchants. The RBI describes a payment gateway as technology that helps route an online transaction without handling the funds. A branded checkout can therefore tell you whose software you see while leaving open who settles your money. RBI’s announcement of the consolidated directions · RBI payments glossary

Write the proposed contracting parties across the top of a sheet. Under each, list the tasks that party agrees to perform. If the proposal names a group brand but the agreement names a subsidiary, ask the provider to connect those names in writing. Ask your adviser to check the authorisation position of the entity performing aggregation for the proposed activity. A sales presentation alone cannot settle that question.

For a platform embedding checkout in its own product, also identify the seller on the invoice. Is the platform selling its own subscription, or is a shop using the platform to sell goods to a shopper? Bring one invoice of each kind if both arrangements exist. The counterparty and the intended recipient of settlement can differ between those two sales.

Find the owner of merchant onboarding

Chapter IV of the RBI Master Direction assigns customer due diligence to the payment aggregator. It addresses the case where one aggregator contracts with another that actually onboards the merchant. It also calls for validation of the merchant’s settlement account and ongoing monitoring against the merchant’s business profile. Chapter V describes the separate escrow account a non-bank aggregator uses for merchant collections. Have your provider and adviser confirm how the applicable provisions fit your arrangement. RBI Master Direction on regulation of payment aggregators

Use the following questions to turn those responsibilities into a procurement discussion. Ask for a sample with personal information removed wherever you need to see evidence.

Ask the provider Evidence to request Who needs the answer
Which entity will onboard this shop? The proposed agreement and onboarding contact Merchant operations
How do you confirm the recipient account? The account verification process and how changes are approved Finance
Where will our trading name and merchant reference appear? A sample transaction record and merchant report Support
Who investigates a payout discrepancy? An example settlement report and the escalation contact Finance and support

One revealing follow-up is to ask what happens when a shop changes its bank account on the afternoon before a payout. Who verifies the request? Which account applies to a batch already in progress? Who tells the merchant when the change takes effect? Record the provider’s answer and identify the contractual or operational document that supports it.

Walk one ₹2,400 order through the proposed setup

Illustrative example. A domestic shop sells a lamp for ₹2,400 through an online storefront. The shopper pays in rupees. A gateway passes payment information to the connected provider, and a non-bank aggregator is responsible for collection and settlement in this example. The shop later authorises a ₹400 partial refund for an accessory it cannot supply. Assume the sale and refund fall in the same batch and ignore fees and other adjustments.

The arithmetic is simple. The harder test is whether three people can independently locate that ₹2,000. Ask the shop to find the original order number. Ask support to find the payment and refund references from that order. Then ask finance to show where those references appear in the batch that explains the bank credit. If the provider combines several orders in one transfer, request the report that breaks that transfer into its constituent entries.

Now move the refund to the following batch. Finance would look for ₹2,400 before fees in the first batch and the ₹400 adjustment in the next. A support agent seeing “refund requested” may be looking at a different point in time from the colleague inspecting a bank statement. Agree which timestamp each report uses and which status supports a customer-facing statement that the refund was issued.

Compare provider answers before comparing prices

Give both shortlisted providers the same fictional order and the same refund date. Invite them to use their own sample reports. An answer such as “our dashboard supports refunds” leaves a merchant with an open question about where the deduction appears. A demonstration that connects the refund reference to a batch and a bank transfer gives the finance team a way to investigate future enquiries.

Read the one-merchant PSP pilot guide if you need a broader launch checklist. The developer guide can help your technical team frame a related question: if the shopper closes the browser after paying, which provider status will the merchant use to decide whether to release the order? Put the technical response beside the settlement evidence so everyone is discussing the same transaction.

Prepare a decision the merchant can sign off

  1. Ask the commercial lead to list the contracting entities, their roles and the exact merchant activity covered by the offer.
  2. Ask operations to confirm who completes onboarding, handles account changes and reviews changes to the shop’s product range.
  3. Ask finance to reconcile the sample purchase and refund and highlight any deductions or time differences that still need explanation.
  4. Have support rehearse one shopper enquiry and one merchant payout enquiry using the proposed contacts and references.

If a provider cannot supply an example report until onboarding is complete, ask for the field names and a sample populated with fictional values. Assign someone to match that sample to the first live report. Before committing to a wider launch, check the unresolved items with the acquirer and your adviser. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a payment gateway and an aggregator in India?

The gateway provides technology to route transactions. A payment aggregator collects customer payments and settles funds to merchants. Ask your provider to identify who performs each function in your proposed arrangement.

Who checks the merchant when two aggregators are involved?

Where one payment aggregator contracts with another that onboards the merchant, the RBI assigns merchant due diligence to the onboarding aggregator. Ask for its legal name and a contact for onboarding questions.

Does the same setup cover international sales?

The RBI distinguishes online, physical and cross-border aggregation. Ask your provider and adviser to review the countries, currencies and movement of funds before expanding a domestic arrangement.
