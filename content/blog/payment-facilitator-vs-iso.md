If you want to earn revenue from card payments without becoming a bank, you will quickly meet two labels: the independent sales organisation and the payment facilitator. The **payment facilitator vs ISO** question matters because the two models look similar from a merchant's point of view, yet they put very different obligations on you.

In short, an ISO *sells* an acquirer's merchant accounts. A payment facilitator *runs* merchant accounts under its own. This guide explains what each model involves in practice, where the risk sits, and how to decide, whether you are joining an [ISO and referral partner program](/partner-program) or planning to operate a gateway of your own.

## What an ISO does

An independent sales organisation (ISO) is a business that an acquirer registers with the card schemes to act on its behalf. Visa's overview of acceptance entities describes an ISO as an agent carrying out activities such as soliciting accounts, sales, customer service and training, and notes that a seller who signs with an ISO is in fact signing with the acquirer.

That one sentence explains most of the model:

- **The merchant agreement is with the acquirer.** The ISO introduces the merchant, helps with the application and often provides first-line support, but each merchant gets its own merchant account.
- **The ISO does not touch settlement.** Funds go from the acquirer or processor to the merchant. The ISO earns a share of the processing margin, known as residuals.
- **Underwriting is the acquirer's decision.** The ISO can prepare a strong file, but it cannot approve a merchant by itself.

Some ISOs take on more: they set pricing, supply terminals or a gateway, and may agree to share in merchant losses in return for a better split. Visa's guidance also notes that an ISO which begins processing transactions falls into a different agent category, so the label follows what you actually do.

## What a payment facilitator does

A payment facilitator, often shortened to payfac, goes a step further. Visa defines it as a third party that signs merchant agreements on the acquirer's behalf and/or receives settlement on behalf of its sponsored merchants. Doing either one makes you a payment facilitator. Mastercard describes it as a service provider registered by an acquirer to facilitate transactions on behalf of submerchants.

In practice that means:

- **You contract with the merchant.** Your sponsored merchants (Mastercard calls them submerchants) sign your terms and are onboarded under your master account, which is why onboarding can take minutes instead of days.
- **You receive and distribute settlement.** The acquirer settles to you, and you pay out each merchant.
- **You do the risk work.** You carry out due diligence at sign-up, monitor transactions for fraud and scheme compliance, and manage disputes. Visa's guidance states that the acquirer must make sure proper due diligence happens when sponsored merchants are signed, and that the payment facilitator must monitor their activity.

There are limits. Visa's published guidance says the acquirer must be added to the merchant agreement once a sponsored merchant's annual Visa sales exceed US $1,000,000 (or the local equivalent), and certain higher-risk categories are treated differently. Rules vary by scheme and change over time, so check the current position with your sponsoring acquirer.

## Payment facilitator vs ISO: side by side

| | ISO | Payment facilitator |
|---|---|---|
| Who signs the merchant | The acquirer (the ISO acts as its agent) | The payment facilitator, on the acquirer's behalf |
| Merchant account | One per merchant | Sponsored merchants sit under a master account |
| Who handles settlement | Acquirer pays the merchant | Payment facilitator receives and distributes funds |
| Underwriting and monitoring | Acquirer decides; the ISO prepares the file | Payment facilitator performs it, overseen by the acquirer |
| Onboarding speed | Depends on the acquirer's process | Can be near-instant, within your risk policy |
| Typical revenue | Residual share of processing margin | Margin on processing, less your risk and operating costs |
| Scheme registration | Registered by the acquirer as an agent | Registered by the acquirer as a payment facilitator |
| Operational burden | Sales, support, portfolio management | All of that plus risk, compliance, payouts and disputes |

## Who carries the risk?

This is the part most comparisons gloss over. Under both models the acquirer remains responsible to the card schemes. Visa's guidance is explicit that acquirers are responsible for the acts of payment facilitators and of sponsored merchants.

Because of that, the acquirer passes obligations down by contract:

- **Payment facilitators** are normally held responsible for losses from their sponsored merchants, such as chargebacks a merchant cannot cover. Expect the acquirer to review your financial strength, policies and systems before sponsoring you, and to ask for reserves or other security. Visa requires the acquirer to complete a risk and financial review of a payment facilitator as part of registration.
- **ISOs** vary. A referral-style ISO usually carries no merchant loss liability. An ISO that shares liability earns more and takes real exposure, so read that clause carefully.

Handling other people's money can also bring obligations outside the card scheme rules, including money transmission and anti-money-laundering requirements that differ by jurisdiction. This article is not legal advice; speak to a regulatory adviser before you design a funds flow.

## How to choose between the two models

Work through these questions in order:

1. **Do you need to control onboarding?** If slow or unpredictable approvals would break your product, as they often do for software platforms, the payment facilitator model or a managed version of it is worth the effort. If your merchants are happy with a normal application, an ISO relationship is simpler.
2. **Do you want to handle funds?** Payouts, split payments and holding reserves need a payment facilitator structure and the controls that go with it.
3. **Can you staff risk and compliance?** Underwriting, monitoring and disputes are daily operations, not a one-off project.
4. **What is your merchant mix?** Larger merchants may outgrow a sponsored arrangement, and specialist categories need acquirers that accept them. See how acquirers assess applications in our guide to [high-risk merchant account underwriting](/blog/high-risk-merchant-account-underwriting).
5. **How fast do you need to launch?** An ISO or referral agreement can be live quickly. A full payment facilitator programme takes longer because the acquirer has to assess and register you.

Many businesses start as a referral partner or ISO, learn their portfolio, then move towards a payment facilitator model for the segments where control pays for itself. Others sit in between by using a provider that carries the registration while they keep the brand and the merchant relationship.
